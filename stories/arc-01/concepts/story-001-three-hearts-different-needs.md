## Story

Ethan loves two very different women, but being a different version of himself with each creates an internal struggle.

## Plot

Morgan expects Ethan to trust her leadership, while Ruby looks to Ethan for reassurance and direction. Both women love him, but Ethan begins feeling that neither fully knows the person he is when he is alone.

## Intended Outcome

Ethan realizes he can love and support both women without surrendering his identity.

individual people with continuing interests and relationships; no child is a
prop, mediator, or explanation for the adults' entire conflict.

### Opening Purpose and Reader Question

Introduce Ethan, Morgan, Ruby, and their family through ordinary interactions
so a first-time reader needs no outside explanation. The first four pages
contrast Ethan's relational roles, reveal the shared family, and end on his
private uncertainty. Establish affection before developing pressure; this
opening is not proposed as a major argument or a jealousy plot.

> Who is Ethan when neither Morgan nor Ruby needs him to be someone?

### Pages 1–4: The Hook

**Page 1 — Morgan.** Morning in the kitchen. Morgan confidently coordinates
the household getting ready to leave; Ethan naturally falls into step with her.
The source suggests a full-page or near-full-page opening followed by a final
panel; the exact layout needs storyboard resolution.

Proposed dialogue, with the child name reconciled as noted above:

> **Morgan:** "Ethan. Shoes. Rowan's looking for his again."
>
> **Ethan:** "Got it."

Morgan is decisive and affectionate. For the final beat, she adjusts his
collar or briefly pulls him toward her; Ethan smiles.

> **Morgan:** "What would I do without you?"

**Page 2 — Ruby.** In the same house, Ruby quietly questions how she handled
an interaction involving a child. The child, incident, and exact location
remain open. Ethan's posture changes: he becomes calm, grounded, reassuring.
Possible staging: kneeling beside her or touching her shoulder.

> **Ruby:** "Do you think I handled that right?"
>
> **Ethan:** "You did fine."
>
> **Ethan:** "She needed you to listen. You listened."

Ruby relaxes. The visual point is the same Ethan occupying a different role;
her uncertainty in this moment must not erase her competence as a parent.

**Page 3 — The family reveal.** Morgan enters naturally, without jealousy.

> **Morgan:** "You two planning on staying in here all morning?"
>
> **Ruby:** "He's giving me a pep talk."
>
> **Morgan:** "Of course he is."

Morgan kisses Ethan on the cheek as she passes; Ruby takes his hand. Children
move around all three adults comfortably. Let affection and shared parenting
communicate that they are a family rather than opening with an explanation
of polyamory. Morgan and Ruby's bond remains platonic.

**Page 4 — Ethan alone.** A mirror in a small private space: bathroom,
bedroom, or garage are alternatives, not selected locations. With nobody
asking for him, his expression settles into quiet exhaustion. The source
does not propose a diagnosis or a claim that he hates his life.

Proposed internal narration, representing Ethan's perception:

> *Morgan trusts me to follow her.*
>
> *Ruby trusts me to lead her.*
>
> *Sometimes I wonder which one of them actually knows me.*
>
> *Sometimes I wonder if I do.*

End on Ethan looking at himself and the chapter title,
**THREE HEARTS, DIFFERENT NEEDS**.

### Pages 5–10: Establish the Family

Slow down and show what Ethan loves about this life. Both relational roles
can be authentic and chosen; their differences are not themselves the problem.
The split between the two exchanges within these six pages remains open.

**Morgan and Ethan:** Show their quick decisions, trust, affection, and joking
disagreement. In a still-unspecified family problem, Morgan proposes a course
of action; Ethan retains the right to challenge her.

> **Morgan:** "We're doing it this way."
>
> **Ethan:** "You know I can still tell you when that's a terrible idea."
>
> **Morgan:** "You usually do."

A small smile keeps the exchange warm. His following her is voluntary.

**Ruby and Ethan:** Show Ruby as capable, nurturing, and perceptive, with
emotional vulnerability rather than helplessness. She reads his silence
differently from Morgan and seeks reassurance.

> **Ruby:** "Are you upset with me?"

Ethan reassures her; the exact reply is not supplied in the source. Let the
reader see the energy he spends being an emotional anchor without presenting
Ruby's needs as wrong or making him her only source of strength.

### Pages 11–15: The Children

Introduce each child with a memorable moment through family interaction,
not an exposition list. Exact individual page assignments remain open.

- **Rowan, 9 — proposed adaptation of the source's eldest-child beat.** He
  notices Ethan has overlooked his own coffee: "Dad, you forgot your coffee."
  Keep "Dad" provisional. A brief observation does not make Rowan a caretaker
  or mediator. Preserve his sociable humor and building/puzzle interests;
  do not adopt the source's Ellie personality description as his new profile.
- **Avery, 7 — proposed middle-child beat.** Give her a moment with Ruby,
  who patiently understands her communication while Ethan watches an approach
  different from his own. Preserve Avery's playful personality, movement/music/
  sports/climbing interests, and preference for comfortable physical or
  nonverbal communication before speech. The exact interaction stays open:
  do not invent an AAC method, welcomed touch, or a sensory trigger. Ruby's
  attentiveness does not change Avery's closest attachment to Ethan.
- **Riley, approximately 6 — proposed adaptation of the youngest-child beat.**
  Give her a funny, lively moment grounded in her existing fearless curiosity,
  pretend play, costumes, cats, or insistence on trying things independently.
  Preserve her closest attachment to Morgan; do not transfer Owen's proposed
  traits or the source's uncertain attachment suggestion into her canon.

The household should feel like a lived-in home. Give the children their own
interests and choices; none diagnoses or repairs the adults' conflict. Preserve
Rowan and Riley's close pairing while keeping Avery included in family life.

### Pages 16–20: The Problem Appears

Morgan and Ruby need Ethan at nearly the same moment. Neither request is
unreasonable; the specific task and topic remain to be chosen.

> **Morgan:** "Come help me with this."
>
> **Ruby:** "Ethan, can I talk to you?"

Ethan hesitates, and both notice. The source tentatively favors his helping
Morgan first; that order remains a proposal, not a settled priority rule.

> **Ruby:** "It's okay."

Ethan understands this as "I'll wait," rather than "it doesn't matter."
Show that as his interpretation, not an objective declaration of Ruby's
unspoken motives. His tension is the constant switching between what he
believes each person needs him to be. No catastrophe is required.

### Pages 21–24: Pressure

Later Ruby gets time to talk. Her concern is his tiredness, not jealousy.

> **Ruby:** "You seem tired."
>
> **Ethan:** "I'm fine."
>
> **Ruby:** "You always say that."

Later Morgan independently notices the same strain, using a more direct voice.

> **Morgan:** "What's wrong with you today?"
>
> **Ethan:** "Nothing."
>
> **Morgan:** "Bullshit."

Keep the tone and profanity open for dialogue review. Both women see something
of Ethan's distress. His deflection keeps them from understanding the whole
picture; neither is framed as incapable of knowing or caring for him.

### Pages 25–27: Ethan's Realization

A rare quiet moment alone, possibly late at night in the kitchen after
everyone is asleep. Three mugs or cups on the table and nearby family photos
are proposed visual motifs, not finalized props or approved photo contents.

He recognizes that following Morgan can bring relief, while being strong
for Ruby can bring purpose. Neither part is fake.

> *Morgan doesn't make me weak.*
>
> *Ruby doesn't make me strong.*
>
> *They're just different parts of me.*
>
> *The problem isn't that they don't know who I am.*
>
> *I've never shown either of them everything.*

This is a proposed emotional turn in Ethan's subjective narration. It begins
self-recognition without declaring him solely responsible for every future
relationship difficulty or requiring him to surrender privacy.

### Pages 28–30: A Small First Step

The next morning, Morgan asks him to do something. Instead of automatically
agreeing, Ethan asks for a little time.

> **Ethan:** "Give me five minutes."

Morgan pauses, surprised rather than angry.

> **Morgan:** "Okay."

Later Ruby asks to speak with him.

> **Ruby:** "Can I ask you something?"
>
> **Ethan:** "Yeah. But I don't know if I'm going to have the answer."
>
> **Ruby:** "I didn't ask you to."

Ruby smiles. These are small proposed changes: a boundary and permission
not to have every answer. They begin Ethan's arc rather than resolving it.
Exact requests, timing, and page division remain open.

### Pages 31–32: The Ending Hook

End with the three adults together, possibly outside after the children are
asleep. Morgan and Ruby talk while Ethan sits between them; Ruby rests against
him and Morgan has her feet across his lap. This is affectionate staging
around Ethan, not a romantic Morgan/Ruby relationship or fixed seating rule.

Ethan belongs and is loved, yet remains thoughtful. Proposed internal narration
and image sequence:

> *I love Morgan.*

Morgan laughing.

> *I love Ruby.*

Ruby smiling, then Ethan.

> *And somewhere between the man they need...*

Final-page close-up of Ethan:

> *...I need to figure out the man I am.*

be curious about Ethan's private uncertainty. The later conflict-heavy stories
can build on that emotional investment.
